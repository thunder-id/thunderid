# Identity Sources and Data

Where consumer identities come from and where they live shape almost everything else about the system. Federation
decides who can bring an existing identity to your application. User stores decide who owns the canonical record once
they arrive.

## Identity Federation

External identity providers, both social and enterprise, let users bring an identity they already have to your
application, instead of managing a separate credential. Done well, federation creates one user record per real person
regardless of how many sign-in methods they end up using. Most consumer apps adopt at least one social provider (Google,
GitHub, and similar), and prosumer apps add enterprise sign-in through OpenID Connect.

There are three main questions that need to be decided here. How does a user record come into existence the first time
someone federates in, and how does a second sign-in method attach to a person who already has one, and does signing out
of your application also end that user's <ProductName /> session, so a returning visit requires federating in again?

### Just-in-Time Provisioning

The first time someone signs in through a provider, no local record exists for them. Just-in-time provisioning creates
one on the spot from whatever the provider returns, so the person gets in without filling out a registration form.

The alternative is to require the record first. Someone invites, imports, or pre-registers the person, or the person has
to create the account by any other means, and federation only signs in people who are already known. Most consumer apps
want the open path. Apps with a paid tier, a waitlist, or a closed beta usually want the gated one, and plenty want
both, open for Google and gated for the enterprise provider.

Provisioning also decides what the new record starts with. A provider usually hands back an email address and a display
name, sometimes a picture. Anything else, a country or a marketing opt-in, has to come from the user after the sign-in.
There is another decision to be made here, whether later sign-ins refresh those attributes, or the local copy is
preserved after it is made, or a mix of these.

### Account Linking

Account linking stops different authenticators creating different identities for one person. So a user who logs in with
email password, Google and GitHub is treated as one person rather than having three different user accounts.

Most apps use different methods to link accounts:

- **Automatic linking on a trusted signal:** the provider returns an identifier that already matches a record, almost
  always a verified email address, and the two join silently. The lowest user-friction of the four, and safe only if the
  provider verifies that signal itself. The downside is an unverified email from a provider that lets anyone claim any
  address is an account takeover waiting to happen.
- **User-confirmed linking:** federation spots the match, then asks the person to confirm the link with the matched
  account through a prompt. They confirm the link, and the link is made. Safer than automatic linking, but still a risk
  if the provider doesn't verify the signal.
- **User-verified linking:** federation spots the match, then defers the link until user verification is complete. The
  person signs in with the original credential or confirms a code sent to the address on file, proving that they are the
  owner of that account. Slower, and the right default for accounts holding payment details or other sensitive
  information.
- **No linking:** every provider identity stays its own record. Simple, and fine when those identities really are
  separate people. Might result in user confusion.

Unlinking deserves the same attention. Decide whether a person can detach a provider themselves, and what happens if
they detach the last sign-in method they have.

Federation can also drive authorization, not just sign-in. An administrator can map attribute values a provider asserts, such as group membership, to local roles, groups, or permissions, so a user's access follows their standing at the provider without being administered a second time inside <ProductName />. See [Federated Identity Provider](../ai-agents/federated-identity) for the full pattern, including how it extends to agents acting on a federated user's behalf, onboarding-time access, and delegating the decision to an external policy engine.

## User Stores

Where consumer identities live affects sign-in performance, recovery options, federation behavior, and migration paths. Most consumer apps run a directly-managed user directory inside the identity product. The other option is no local record at all, relying entirely on federation for identity.

The choice comes down to who owns the canonical record. A directly-managed directory gives you the most features and the simplest operational model: profile attributes, credentials, and recovery state all live in one place. Relying entirely on federation removes that local record, at the cost of depending on the federated provider staying available and accurate for every sign-in.
